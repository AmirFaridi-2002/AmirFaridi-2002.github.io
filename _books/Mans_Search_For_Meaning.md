> “Everything can be taken from a man but one thing: the last of the human freedoms—to choose one’s attitude in any given set of circumstances, to choose one’s own way.”

This sentence was the initial spark that drew me to this book. It is a logical, yet strikingly bold claim; but what I wanted to understand was how this idea would unfold within the book itself. How would it connect to the question of life’s meaning? And what, precisely, does Frankl mean when he speaks of the “meaning of life”?

Ultimately, perhaps the most significant effect this book had on me was that it challenged the way I think about suffering. It suggested that we are not defined solely by the events we have endured, but also by the manner in which we confront them. Perhaps the right question is not what I want from the world, but what the world asks of me. Some time ago, I found myself thinking about the sentence “the world owes me.” Beyond whatever meaning it may or may not have, I wondered whether it was even the right way to frame life. After reading this book, “I owe the world” seems to me a far more meaningful and truthful sentence.

The perspective Viktor Frankl offers in the first part of the book, drawn from his experiences in the Auschwitz concentration camp, could, at the most superficial level, be reduced in the modern mind to statements such as “there are greater sufferings” or “there is always something darker.” But for me, the book does not remain at that level. As it progresses, it invites the reader into a kind of inner exploration: to return to past sufferings, to the events we may still be carrying somewhere in the background of our minds, and to ask whether they can be seen from another angle. Or, in Frankl’s own terms, whether meaning can be found in them.

One of the most compelling ideas Frankl develops throughout both parts of the book is that there is no single, universal meaning of life. Meaning varies from person to person, and even from one moment to another.

According to the school of thought Frankl introduces, meaning in life can be discovered in three ways. We do not force meaning. We do not create meaning. We do not choose meaning. We discover it.

1. By creating or accomplishing something
2. By encountering something or someone
3. By the attitude we adopt toward unavoidable pain and suffering

The first path is relatively straightforward. The second way of finding meaning involves encountering values such as goodness, truth, and beauty through nature and culture, or coming to know another human being through love. This part reminds me of Nietzsche’s famous line: “We have art in order not to die of the truth.” Loving, too, is a form of art.

But the idea I connected with most deeply was the possibility of finding meaning in life’s unavoidable suffering. This is where the quotation I began with became clear to me. Even when we find ourselves in difficult circumstances, facing a fate that can no longer be changed, there remains one thing that cannot be taken from us: the freedom to choose our attitude toward what has happened. We may not be able to change the event itself, but we can still choose how to face it, and what we allow it to make of us.

What matters, then, is each person’s unique relationship to their own suffering and the way they bear it. Under certain conditions, even a tragedy can become a form of victory; a painful situation can be transformed into a human achievement.

And the example Frankl gives from his counseling experience to illustrate this point is remarkable:

“Once, an elderly general practitioner consulted me because of his severe depression. He could not overcome the loss of his wife who had died two years before and whom he had loved above all else. Now, how can I help him? What should I tell him? Well, I refrained from telling him anything but instead confronted him with the question, “What would have happened, Doctor, if you had died first, and your wife would have had to survive you?” “Oh,” he said, “for her this would have been terrible; how she would have suffered!” Whereupon I replied, “You see, Doctor, such a suffering has been spared her, and it was you who have spared her this suffering — to be sure, at the price that now you have to survive and mourn her.” He said no word but shook my hand and calmly left my office. In some way, suffering ceases to be suffering at the moment it finds a meaning, such as the meaning of a sacrifice.”
