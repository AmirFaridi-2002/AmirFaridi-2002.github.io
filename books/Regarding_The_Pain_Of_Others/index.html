Regarding the Pain of Others

Susan Sontag  ·  2003

 ·   ·   · 

 ·  war-photography   visual-culture   ethics   journalism  ·  nonfiction   essays   photography   media-criticism   war   history


These dead are supremely uninterested in the living: in those who took their lives; in witnesses—and in us. Why should they seek our gaze? What would they have to say to us? “We”—this “we” is everyone who has never experienced anything like what they went through—don’t understand. We don’t get it. We truly can’t imagine what is was like. We can’t imagine how dreadful, how terrifying war is; and how normal it becomes. Can’t understand, can’t imagine. That’s what every soldier, and every journalist and aid worker and independent observer who has put in time under fire, and had the luck to elude the death that struck down others nearby, stubbornly feels. And they are right.

A note before “what this book is about”

Chapter 5 is the pivot where Sontag pulls the camera back.

Instead of starting with modern photojournalism and our familiar moral arguments (“Do these images wake us up or numb us?”), she asks a prior question: why do we assume war images are supposed to shock us in the first place?

Her answer begins with a historical reset. The modern moral instinct treats war as a rupture in normal life—an aberration, even if it keeps recurring. But for most of history, she argues, the baseline was the opposite: war was the normal condition and peace the exception. Once you accept that older premise, “graphic” description looks less like transgression and more like craft.

She threads this through art and literature:

  • In the Iliad, the gruesome detail is not gratuitous; it is part of how epic storytelling works. It culminates. It lingers. It insists.
  • In Renaissance battle painting (she quotes Leonardo’s instructions), the demand is for concreteness: twisted limbs, clenched teeth, dust, blood—enough detail that the scene cannot be softened into pageantry.
  • And in the long tradition after that, atrocity has been rendered not only to document, but also to create a kind of terrible aesthetic charge—what she calls a “challenging” beauty that depends on detachment as much as on compassion.

By the end of the chapter, her critique lands in a very contemporary place: even an honest, historically broad education about a country’s wars—especially one that includes the photographic record of what weapons do—can be treated as disloyal. The problem is not the existence of images; the problem is the social permission to look at them without being told what to feel, and what conclusions are “allowed.”

So what is Regarding the Pain of Others really about?

This book is Sontag’s extended meditation on what it means to look at images of suffering—especially war photographs—and to believe that looking is a form of witnessing.

Across its short, numbered chapters, she circles a set of stubborn questions:

  • What do photographs of atrocity actually do to the viewer: provoke empathy, provoke action, provoke cynicism, or simply become another consumable spectacle?
  • Why do we treat photographs as evidence, and what kinds of truth do they not supply (context, causes, responsibility, the lived interior of pain)?
  • How much of what we “see” in an image is carried by captions, narratives, and the political frames around it?
  • What is the difference between remembering through images and understanding through history?

Sontag doesn’t argue that war images are useless—she argues against the easy hopes we place in them. Photographs can stop us, haunt us, and sometimes change public conversation. But they can also be absorbed, repeated, aestheticized, instrumentalized, and forgotten. The ethical challenge is not only to feel something when confronted with pain, but to think clearly about the conditions under which that feeling becomes knowledge—or becomes noise. And if you read this book to help your sense of what is happening—inside an image, inside a narrative, and inside your own response, it does its job.
